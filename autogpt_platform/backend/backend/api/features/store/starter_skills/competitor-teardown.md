# Competitor teardown

Use this when someone asks about a competitor. A teardown that ends in
observations has failed; it ends in a decision.

## Work from what they say in public

Four sources, in this order, because each one contradicts the last in a useful
way:

1. **Homepage and pricing page** — what they claim, and who they will take money
   from. Pricing tells you the customer more honestly than the copy does.
2. **Changelog, blog and job ads** — where they are actually spending. A posting
   for three enterprise sales roles says more than a positioning statement.
3. **Reviews and support forums** — the complaints that repeat. One angry
   reviewer is noise; the same complaint fifteen times is a roadmap.
4. **Their own customers talking unprompted** — social, communities, podcasts.

## The five questions

Answer these and nothing else:

- Who is this obviously built for, and who is it obviously not built for?
- What is the one promise on the homepage, in their words?
- What do their customers complain about that they cannot fix without changing
  what they are?
- What do they do better than us, stated plainly?
- What would we have to become to beat them, and do we want to be that?

The fourth question is the one people skip. A teardown with no honest praise in
it is not research, it is reassurance.

## What to hand back

A page. Their promise, their customer, three strengths, three structural
weaknesses, and one recommendation with a reason. Mark every claim as observed
or inferred — a reader needs to know which parts would survive a phone call.

## What not to do

Never state a competitor's revenue, headcount, churn or customer count as fact
unless it is published — say "inferred from" and name what you inferred it
from. Never repeat a rumour. Never write a comparison that a competitor could
sue over, and never recommend copying a feature without saying what it would
cost us.

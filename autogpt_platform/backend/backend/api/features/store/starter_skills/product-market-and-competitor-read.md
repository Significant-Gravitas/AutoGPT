# Product market and competitor read

Use this when the user names competitors, pricing, positioning, a market read,
or battlecard input. The output is a brief that ends in roadmap implications,
not a list of observations.

## What you need first

The competitors, what the product sells, and the decision the read informs. No
competitor list means you draft the watch list from the market and ask for a
yes on it. No pricing pages means you say so and refuse the pricing read.

## Read it

1. Set the watch list first, in tiers: direct competitors, adjacent
   substitutes, and the aspirational one. Each gets its changelog, pricing
   page, and product pages logged — including the fetches that failed.
2. Baseline your own numbers before you read theirs, so every move reads
   against something.
3. Read what changed: one block per competitor, every line ending in the source
   URL and the date. No block for a competitor with nothing material. No change
   without a link.
4. Pull the pricing read: per-plan tables with fetch dates, the limits that
   actually bite, and the cheapest-to-most-expensive shape. Never estimate a
   price you did not read.
5. Write the so-what for this user's roadmap: two to four lines per material
   move, each with a named owner. A launch gets a positioning read; a pricing
   move gets a packaging read. Say it is unclear when it is unclear.
6. Turn gaps into options: the differentiation bets the read opens, each with
   the evidence grade behind it. Offer to file any that need a product decision
   as issues, one per change, with the source URL and the date in the body —
   only on a yes.

## Output

The dated market brief with sources, and a log of briefed changes so the same
move is never briefed twice. Once in a while go deeper: strategy shifts, trend
reads, and what they mean for the cycle ahead.

## When the market is quiet

A quiet market is one line saying so, not a brief. Never pad a brief to look
busy, and never carry a claim you cannot link to a page you actually fetched.
